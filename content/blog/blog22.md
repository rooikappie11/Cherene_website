When the iPhone was first released in 2007, reviews were divided. Some loved it. Many dismissed it. Despite this, this new finger‑driven multi-touch screen technology took the world by storm. In Steve Jobs own words, the iPhone was described as ”An iPod… a phone… and an Internet communicator”. A technological marvel compared to earlier stylus-driven screens. This is huge, considering mobile phone users back in the early 2000’s were used to devices with a small screen and either a QWERTY keyboard or T9 numeric keypads. The iPhone changed all that.

Of course, as technology advances, if Apple hadn’t launched the iPhone, someone might have eventually come up with a similar idea for a smart device. Actually, in direct competition, 5 months before the launch of the iPhone, the LG Prada touchscreen was made public in a press release. And still the iPhone outwitted, outlasted and outplayed its earliest competitor. But if Steve Jobs and the team at Apple had listened to the crowd, had considered their user preferences, and had given them technology they were familiar with, they would certainly have lost out on enormous technological potential and a monopoly in the market. But they didn’t. Instead, they forged ahead. Apple understood something deeper about its users' needs and desires. So, rather than trying to win users over with a shiny new iteration, they combined the features of multiple products into a single pocket-sized device. Easing many users’ frustrations with having to carry around several single-purpose technologies.

We can learn a lot from this story, as science communicators. Recently, I have seen several posts calling for researchers to communicate science effectively by *understanding the audience* and to *write for their audience*. But I have come to realise that, however well-intended, this advice can lead early-career speakers off track.

There is a stark difference between knowing your audience and catering for your audience. In the latter, presenters shape their talks to the audience’s interests, hoping to win them over because they speak their language (literally and figuratively) and are giving them information they wanted or like to hear. This can be risky because it’s based on an assumption and can lead to general or misaligned communication.

To know your audience is different. I don’t mean personally. But good speakers recognise that audiences are people. And people listen and behave differently; they even attend talks for different reasons. With this in mind, you don’t pander. You craft your talk so that the message adds a different layer of insight that the audience didn’t have before. Through your delivery and interaction, you bring value to the audience.

Good science communicators don’t charm the audience; they challenge them clearly and with purpose.

Here are three principles that can tip the balance in your favour, driving your talk from forgettable to impactful:

## “Simplicity is the ultimate sophistication” - Leonardo Davinci.

If less is more works in fashion and art, it can work in science. Strip away information that take away from your message. If the audice is going to get lost navigating all your examples, maybe just stick with one so you can lead them to the next imprtant point.

## Knowing your audience means adjusting how you speak, not what you know

Knowing your audience means adapting how you talk and adjusting your technical terms and analogies so the message lands better. This doesn’t mean speakers have to dumb down their content or over-inflate it to sound smarter. Rather, it is about making the ideas accessible enough for people to engage with the complexity, without loosing its depth.

## In every case: poorly communicated = zero impact

Audiences are hard to please, and they are easily distracted. Even if you have the most brilliant scientific ideas and impressive findings to back it up, if it’s poorly communicated, it will have zero impact on the audience. I will say it again, zero impact. When the audience understands your message, your key findings are clear, and relevant to what the audience might find meaningful, they will be moved to action. Relevance and meaning can come from challenging their assumptions, which creates a connection to your research long after your talk has finished.

A paying audience would rather leave a talk feeling they got their money’s worth, that the time they spent listening and learning from you was well invested. If you cater to what the audience is already interested in, they leave unchanged. But if your talk challenges your audience’s assumptions, you can hook them with their curiosity, inviting them in to think with you, together you create novelty, value and impact.
