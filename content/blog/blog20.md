Last weekend, I made the hour journey to Manchester to attend my very first TEDx talk. To say I was excited in an understatement. This year’s programme included a diverse group of speakers from a Neuroscientist, a Magician Scientist, an Entrepreneur, a Productivity Guru and an Archaeologist. It was an insightful day, and I left Manchester more excited and energised about the ideas. But importantly, I left with a notebook full of tips on becoming a great communicator.

In this post, I will point out the 10 things I observed that make a good talk, providing you with the tools and tips used and tested by the greats of TEDx. So, without further ado, let’s jump in.

## Excitement, not anxiety, is key
Of the bat, every speaker that stepped onto the stage to take a position on the Big Red Dot oozed with excitement. It was obvious in the way they walked and talked. If there were any nerves, I don’t think they showed it. As soon as their feet touched the stage, they were happy to be there, and we, the audience, could feel that electricity burning off the stage.

For PhD students, like me, this reminds me that my attitude before and during the talk is important. If I signal from the start to myself and the audience that what I have to share is interesting, the audience will naturally be curious and want to listen. There is a fine line between feeling anxious and excited.


## The show must go on
Technology is a wonderful tool, but sometimes it can let us down. And TEDx is no exception. During one of the talks, the speaker wasn’t able to connect to the Wi-Fi for a demonstration (Bummer, I know..). While the speaker was a little disappointed (maybe even embarrassed?), he didn’t falter. He continued his talk with passion and pizzazz. Drawing the audience in and keeping their attention. In PhD talks, things can go awry. I have seen talks disturbed by fire alarms and power cuts, speakers having the wrong slides and even water spilt on the podium. Hiccups on stage will happen, but even in these sticky situations, we should muster up the courage and let the show go on.

## It’s a tool, not the focus
I think what makes TEDx talks so hard, and their speakers such excellent communicators, is that they need to deliver their talk without the need for excessive props or slide decks. The talk, their stage presence, and the message are the main focus. Slides and props are used, but as secondary tools, only to aid in explaining specific concepts and ideas. PowerPoint presentations are not the mechanism through which the talk is delivered. Which means no Death by PowerPoint or slides with excessive text. I wonder if this is something we can do in Academia. Can we talk about our research, out is significance without texts, using slides sparingly to show exciting finds, photos or graphs? Something to try at the next conference, I think.

## Mixing it up
What I noticed from the range of this year’s speakers is that many of them transitioned from Academic backgrounds into industry. What inspired me about these speakers is how easy they blend scientific ideas into concepts that a broader public could understand. It was done elegantly. The ideas were simplified into digestible chunks without dumbing down the meaning or significance. They managed to capture the perfect blend of technical and general understanding, meaning the rigour of the research, but signalling value in a way that the audience could relate. I think we often wrongly assume as students, that to sound smart you need to express ideas in its most complex form, especially at conferences. But I think what really matters is rather showing how these complex research questions, experiments and ideas are valuable and relevant to the field. Not everyone in the audience is necessarily an expert in your research, so it’s our job as the speaker to find a way to relate it to an aspect that every audience member will be curious about. And TEDx proved this is something academics are capable of.

## The power of Storytelling
To capture attention, speakers need to connect with the audience. This can be done through a memory, a sound, a habit or a feeling – anything that the audience can relate to. Personal stories or experiences are a great tool to enhance a talk. As researchers, we don’t often train ourselves to use the power of storytelling when we communicate our science. It’s a shame, really, since we are solving society’s problems but fail to connect to those same communities. Finding common ground, like a shared experience, is a good way to build relationships. And a talk is nothing more than an opportunity for a researcher to build a bridge between other research ideas and communities with shared interests.

Using storytelling is like a golden threat that allows the speaker to connect experiences to the audience to hold their attention.
Tickle the funny bone (but not too much)
Another way to connect to the audience is through humour, but it is a tricky one and should be used sparingly. Used at the wrong moment, it can distract from the purpose of the talk. If you don’t land the punchline quite right, the audience might miss the joke entirely. It’s even more difficult to start with a joke right from the start because the speakers do not necessarily have rapport with the audience. I don’t think any of the speakers opened with a joke. I do remember laughing during the talk and even sometimes when they finished. Jokes were used sparingly, saved for moments to indicate a change or lighten the mood. They were timely and made sense within the context of the different talks.

## Grab their attention
Plot twists are amazing tools fiction writers use to introduce change and sometimes growth. In a talk, it builds anticipation and surprise, drawing and keeping the audience’s attention and focus. It’s a good method to use if you have unexpected results, something interesting occurred during your experiment, which led to a shift in the question or approach. Plot twists in research, I think, can signal exploration. Like something unexpected happened in the results, it might even be a happy accident, something that occurred serendipitously – it’s a discovery, nonetheless. So use it to grab the audience’s attention.

## Information overload – It’s a no-no
A 20-minute talk seems like a long time. But it goes by quickly once the speakers are in the groove. Considering that TEDx speakers have to compete for the audience’s attention, sustained over an entire day’s programme and other equally interesting talks, is a lot to ask. The trick I observed from speakers was to keep it simple. Many speakers had no more than three main take-aways of key points. Three is a good number because it is easy to remember. By only having 3 points, speakers don’t ramble and they don’t overload the audience. Added bonus of limiting yourself to 3 key points is that you can use the rest of the time to pause, to breathe. Having these strategic breaks shows the audience you are confident, it creates stage presence, but it also allows the audience to catch up and think with you.

## Make it stick
If you want your ideas to forever be embedded into the frontal lobes of the audience, the tip: use a clever keyword or phrase. Something that is short, sweet and easy to remember. In academia, recognition is important. Having a clever phrase will spark further curiosity and interest. It will also be something people associate with you and your research. Leaving the audience with this little nugget will make them feel as if they learned something valuable, especially when they can remember it. It is also a good conversation starter for the awkward moments around the conference sandwich table.

## Before you hang up
The last trick is probably one of the most recommended by expert speakers and communication gurus around the world. After telling your story, after delivering your punchline, and your last key takeaway, close your talk with a Call to Action. This might be difficult in Academia, but it is not impossible. It could be a call for collaborators, a call for shared data, maybe a future session panelled by PhD students or a further discussion during the coffee break. Whatever it is, share your ideas, but remember to make the audience part of the process by allowing them to take part.

## Final Thoughts
To excel in good communication, I think these 10 tips should be a part of any researcher's arsenal. They are a powerful aid to explore science, show value and encourage future partnerships. However, I did notice that none of the speakers at TEDx consistently used the same tips or even all of them. The advice that probably trumps them all is to use the tips in different combinations, but not everything all at once. A great talk combines 3-4 in a strategic manner to help with the flow of the story, to connect with the audience and to signal the main meaning in the message.
