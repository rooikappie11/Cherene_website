In July of this year (2026), I saw a post on Reddit where a final-year PhD student was seeking advice on getting their thesis done. This particular student was canvassing the PhD community for tips and tricks on how to convert the data and analyses completed and collected into written works for the final thesis. The problem wasn’t the data itself or a lack of clarity or understanding of the results. Rather, the student may have been feeling overwhelmed by the amount of work still ahead, making it difficult to find the energy and momentum needed to bring their PhD to completion. When I visited the site the first time, the post had 119 comments. Fast forward to November 2026, the post now has 199 comments.

![Reddit](/blog/blog32a.png) <br>
Source: [*Reddit*](https://www.reddit.com/r/PhD/comments/1v9yvzi/starting_my_final_phd_year_with_only_10_pages/)<br>

What drew my attention to the post was a sense of camaraderie and an understanding of the final-year panic that can set in, driven by the desire to finish the PhD strong. While I might not be in the same shoes as this student, I, like many PhD students, can relate. Collecting data, finalising experiments and running the last analysis are crucial parts of PhD research. But they require sustained and focused effort, which means time might not always be dedicated to writing. 

A review of the comments on the Reddit post reveals a flurry of well-intended but generic advice such as: hunker down and just write; bribe yourself to complete tasks; set personal deadlines and stick to them; write 500 words a day; any writing is better than no writing. While this is useful advice that has definitely pulled many PhD students through, it’s not advice that can lead to sustainable progress or the development of a balanced work ethic in the long term.

Throughout the course of the PhD, PGRs constantly work towards their research milestones. They undoubtedly face challenges along the way but solve them creatively. But as one of the most important deadlines of the PhD journey looms, many are struggling to make progress, feeling like all the wheels have just stopped turning. In academic environments, progress doesn’t always collapse because researchers are lazy. Increased pressure, cognitive load, and burnout, paired with the insecurity of sustainable academic careers, work together to slow PGR momentum and derail progress. And this fragmentation happens faster than getting access to the systems meant to support them. If you are reading this, you have either been there or are currently there. And it’s ok. All hope is not lost.

The 3-Step Momentum Rebuild Protocol

The quote at the start of this post is from a letter sent by Vincent van Gogh to his brother, Theo, in October 1882. It emphasised that true success is achieved by patient and consistent step-by-step practice, instead of relying on a sudden burst of energy and focus. To echo the consistent step-by-step progress mentioned by Van Gogh, I am proposing a 3-Step Momentum Rebuild Protocol. It is designed to help PhD students take back their agency in the final year and to get the important things done. Instead of consisting of quick tips, this protocol is designed to help you position yourself strategically to gain back your momentum. If you follow the 3 steps, you will identify the barriers that block you from making progress and figure out what loads and responsibilities you have committed to, so that you can be in a position to set what I am calling ‘momentum anchors’ (small, reliable actions), in place. A little caveat - it is still up to you to get the work done. Unfortunately, there is no magic recipe for success. It boils down to self-discipline and consistency.

This 3-Step Momentum Rebuild Protocol is intended for anyone who needs to jump-start their momentum, whether it, is after a leave of absence, the completion of a big project or a well-deserved break.

What you will need:

- Single piece of A4 paper
- A selection of good pens

Divide the paper into three columns. In each column, write the following headings:

1. Truth Audit: What is holding me back?
2. Load Ledger: What am I carrying?
3. Momentum Anchors: What small actions will move me forward?

*An example is attached at the end of this post*

## Step 1: The Truth Audit

To make progress on a task, it is important to identify the root cause that is slowing you down or delaying your progress. The first step is, as such, a reflective practice where we audit our physical space and emotional wellbeing.

Physically, we can look at our environment and the spaces we use. We need to assess whether the space we are setting up to work is fit for purpose. If it isn’t, it might be one of the things that is stopping us from getting work done. Next, we need to reflect and audit ourselves to determine our emotional state. Sometimes our physical environment is already set up to be conducive to good work, but our mental well-being, the insecurities or lies we carry around and tell ourselves, derail our progress. In the truth audit, you need to be honest about how you feel, so that you can start recognizing signs of fear, fatigue, and self-doubt. This is important for acknowledging your feelings and current state of being, recognizing that they are completely normal. By acknowledging, you take back your agency and path to productivity. In the first column, make a bullet list of the things hindering your work physically, emotionally, and mentally.

## Step 2: The Load Ledger

Next, in the middle, or second column, identify what projects you have on your plate and what projects you have agreed to complete. Be specific; write down the project, your role, and the deadline. This helps identify the load of work/tasks you have committed to. After you’ve done this, you might realise, like many of us, you have more projects on your plate than the time and capacity available to complete them. In writing each one down, you start making all the invisible burdens you carry visible. 

In your ledger, you will need to do some prioritizing. Not all projects and tasks are equally urgent or important. Some can be downgraded to make space for more important ones. Reshuffle your projects and tasks using a different colour pen and numbers. While not a hard-and-fast rule, I would recommend having no more than two major projects actively competing for your time and attention. Additionally, around 3–5 urgent and important tasks can be included, but should be tackled one at a time, if possible. 

PhD students can use the load ledger to note down each of their PhD chapters as individual tasks, and mark them as important. When you think about it, a PhD is one overarching project that contains several substantial workstreams, such as ethics applications, laboratory work, data analysis, chapter writing, and thesis preparation. These can be broken down into manageable tasks and prioritised according to their urgency and importance, based on your PhD timeline and individual milestones. 

The goal of this exercise is to recognise your capacity, make deliberate choices about where to direct your energy, and avoid spreading yourself too thin. Everything else that doesn’t qualify as either important or urgent, at this stage, can and should be ignored until you have more time and capacity to tackle them. Now that you are clear on which projects and tasks you can work on, you can start putting practices - or anchors - in place.

## Step 3: The Momentum Anchors

In the third and final column on your paper, for each of your important/urgent tasks, write down the milestones that will contribute to completing the project successfully. If you are like me, and the PhD is one of your projects, it will have several urgent and important tasks. It might look similar to mine: a) Complete the final lab work (November 2026), b) Data analysis for Chapter 3 (December 2026), c) Editing the Chapter 4 draft (November 2026), and d) Prepare for final submission (June 2027).

For each of these milestones, decide on one practice, a small, reliable action, that you can do daily to make sure you make consistent progress and maintain forward momentum even during difficult and stressful periods. This could be getting to the office or library an hour earlier than usual, joining a writing group (online or in-person), scheduling meetings with your supervisor in advance so that the dates of specific target outcomes and commitments are in your calendar already (this is a good way to stay accountable). Time blocking your day is another good way to develop a sustainable routine with daily deep work sessions. I also find that having defined start and shutdown times is a good way to manage the day and keep my focus. Lastly, it might be work, including a lunch break, and thinking walks for fresh air.

The final year of a PhD can bring immense pressure: expectations to deliver, deadlines to meet, and the nagging fear of falling short or not making it to the finish line. Not to mention the stress of finding work after finishing the PhD. But feeling overwhelmed doesn’t mean you’re incapable of finishing. Sometimes, rebuilding momentum starts with something much smaller: taking stock of the physical and emotional barriers holding you back, being honest about what you can realistically take on, and then putting small, consistent daily actions in place. Everything doesn’t have to be completed at once. Instead, it is important to keep moving, one deliberate step at a time. Big projects, like a PhD thesis, aren’t completed in a single burst of energy and motivation, but through the cumulative effort of consistent daily actions.
