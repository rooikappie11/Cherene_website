The jump from a master’s student to a PhD researcher is a significant one.
It requires students to stop treating the doctoral project as a series of assignments and start thinking like investigators.
This shift happens the moment a PhD student stops asking, “Is this what my supervisor wants?”
and instead asks, “What is the real problem here?”
Whether they realise it or not, this is the point at which they cross from compliance (performing tasks) to curiosity (pursuing understanding).

### Why We Default to Compliance

The students’ instinct to check whether they are doing things “correctly” is not a personal failing.
It is a trait inherited by default from previous academic training.
It’s an imprint of a curriculum that was designed to test competence.
It is a structural residue left over by earlier educational systems, convincing students that to succeed, they must deliver assignments efficiently, correctly, and in ways that please evaluators.
Academically performing students learn to meet the expectations of the course instructor or lecturer rather than generating insight from additional readings and their own thinking.
And it is understandable.
It’s the behaviour that previous academic environments rewarded.
But is a behaviour that prevents students from jumping from Master’s to PhD to fully step into the researcher’s shoes.

This is something [Larry McEnerney](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vtIzMaLkCaM&t=1s) also notes as a major challenge for early-career academics and PhD students who are struggling to write and publish papers.
The problem is that students have only ever learned to write for lecturers or module leaders, for people who are paid to care about student writing.
Journal editors and reviewers are not, and it’s ultimately the responsibility of the writer to translate the novelty and value of the work into a form they (readers, reviewers, editors) will appreciate and value.
The problem is that students learn to write in ways that demonstrate competence in a topic rather than showing genuine curiosity or critical thinking.
Very few develop the skills needed to challenge and change thinking within their field, leaving them trapped in assignment‑mode thinking and writing when they enter a PhD.
A PhD is an independent study.
PhDs are still expected to become competent in a specific subject or method; the key difference is that they are also required to show insight.
This is where the main distinction between a PhD and other postgraduate degrees lies.
To break this path dependency, where students’ current behaviour is shaped by past structures and incentives, they must step into a new mode of thinking.

### GRAVES as a Researcher Mindset and Mental Model

In the toolkit of the archaeologist is the ability (based on experience and knowledge) to discern the shape and type of [tool used to dig a pit or burial based on an imprint](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/20548923.2016.1229916) of the tool that has been left on the wall or floor.
Because of these imprints, they can reconstruct the steps and methods used by a specific group to dig a grave, to gain insight about past behaviour and beliefs.
Similarly, because students have assignment-mode imprinted in their brains as a model for successful submission, it is possible to evaluate the tool marks left behind by this assignment mode to identify and change the mental models that are hindering student development as postgraduate researchers.

To produce meaningful research, PhD students need a different system, one that prioritises depth, originality, and long-term contribution over short-term performance.
For this reason, I have developed the GRAVES model.
I first presented the GRAVES model at the Liverpool John Moores University Research Knowledge Exchange Conference1 in 2025, based on my own research requirements, project and PhD journey, as a framework that can be used to think about future impact and real-world implementation of PhD research.

The GRAVES model is not a checklist.
Rather, as discussed here, it’s a way of thinking to help PhD students transition from compliance to curiosity.
It’s a structure that helps PhD students recognise when they have moved beyond assignment‑mode and into genuine inquiry.
Each component represents a habit that supports intentionally focused work and protects students from the distractions that derail so many PhDs.
While each element can be treated both as a mindset shift and as a technical requirement for projects, they work best when these intentions are kept separate.
Their real power emerges when they are positioned to shape and lead behavioural and cognitive change in a PhD student.

### GRAVES: Six researcher mindset shifts

#### G – Grounded Questions 

Focused research begins when the student stops asking, “What question is safe or expected?”
and starts asking, “What question matters in the real world of my field?”
This is the shift from performing to investigating, where the questions anchor the attention and intention of the project.
The right questions, carefully curated, set the foundation for a project that will emphasise both original contribution and the value of the research.

#### R – Robust Methods

Instead of choosing methods because they are familiar or supervisor-approved, the student chooses and tests methods because they are fit for purpose.
The right methods stem from asking the right questions.
This is the shift from convenience to rigour.
It also signals that the student is thinking about how experiments can be validated to ensure reliability.

#### A – Allied 

Thinking While a PhD is an independent study, doctoral research cannot be done in solitary.
To expose blind spots, assumptions and biases, PhD students should seek advice and insight from supervisors, practitioners, peers, and other disciplines.
This is the shift from isolation to intellectual partnership, showing a drive for fostering collaborative capacity-building and knowledge exchange.
It shows that students are thinking about the impact of their research beyond academia.
About how, and by whom, it will be used in the real world.

#### V – Value in Practice 

For research to have an impact, it needs to be meaningful and have value in the real world.
Thinking about the value of the research about “Who benefits from this knowledge?”
allows the PhD student to articulate why their work matters and who it serves.
This is the shift from abstraction to impact; it shapes the intellectual focus of the student and gives them confidence to pursue the project because they can see the tangible and intangible impacts, giving clarity about the project’s significance and relevance.

#### E – Effective Translation

Novel ideas and refined methods are only useful if they can be understood and used by other researchers.
Even if studies are preliminary or part of pilot projects, if the students consider how methods can be transferable across geographic and scientific borders, they are thinking about future implementation.
In the spirit of making research more inclusive and diverse, this allows PhD students to turn ideas into tools, frameworks, or insights that others can use and build on.

#### S – Sustainable Research

Research is cumulative and communal.
It needs to build on current knowledge rather than duplicating what’s already been done.
Sustainability also goes hand in hand with ethical resource use.
By considering the long‑term contribution over short term output, the PhD student, together with the supervisor, can lead research groups to continue research beyond the initial project, ensuring that current investments in equipment or networks are sustained and can be built on in future initiatives.

## **What the Shift Looks Like in Real PhD Students**

The shift from compliance to curiosity is a process, but it requires intentionality.
It becomes visible when PhD students reflect on conversations with supervisors and then reframe their methods; when they take initiative, abandon convenient approaches, and explore alternatives.

Breakthroughs often occur in the “adjacent possible”, a concept borrowed from the work of [Stuart Kauffman](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nEtATZePGmg) and popularised by [Steven Johnson](https://adjacentpossible.substack.com/about), and used by Cal Newport in his book [So Good They Can’t Ignore You](https://www.amazon.co.uk/Good-They-Cant-Ignore-You/dp/1455509124).
The “adjacent possible” is the area just beyond the current cutting edge of a field.
It’s at these borders between disciplines and perspectives where thinkers and explorers figure out new ways to view the world or adapt tools in innovative ways.
Very few students are brave enough to explore these edges.
Most favour the middle, where it feels comfortable and safe.
And assignment mode feels safe because students have been duped into thinking it’s the system that allows them to succeed.
Unfortunately, it won’t in PhD research.

## **Why This Mindset Matters for the PhD Journey**

The GRAVES mental model protects students from common PhD traps such as distractions, topic drift, perfectionism, and overambition.
It fosters a space where the student can stop performing and start investigating.
At the interdisciplinary edges of the adjacent possible, PhD students will need to connect this mindset shift to long-term success and well-being.
As a framework, I think this metal model makes the research more meaningful and more coherent, and the PhD student more resilient to inevitable setbacks, because it encourages a focused approach rooted not in immediate gains but in long-term growth and more valuable research.
The reality is that compliance will get a PhD student through milestones, but only curiosity cultivates insight.
When PhD students adopt these six researcher mindset shifts, they stop performing and start investigating.
And that is the moment they become researchers.
