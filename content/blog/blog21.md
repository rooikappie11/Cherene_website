In comedy, the punchline is the final statement of a story or joke meant to deliver a climactic finale. It is delivered with wit and humour meant to make people laugh or gasp at an unexpected twist or shift in narrative, challenging assumptions.

Like a punch to the gut, the world has been through an emotional, social and political roller coaster. The world feels increasingly chaotic. Now more than ever, it needs problem solvers who are not only intelligent but kind and hopeful. Different kinds of leaders who can relate, who can listen, and who can truly connect.

Over the last couple of weeks, the punchline to humanity’s self‑obsessed notions was delivered by a small [macaque](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/feb/23/punch-monkey-japan-macaque-why-do-mother-animals-abandon-offspring) monkey named Punch‑kun, Punch for short, who is an inhabitant of the Ichikawa City Zoo in Japan. Punch stole hearts across the world when he went viral on social media. 
After birth, Punch was rejected and abandoned by his mother. He has endured bullying from other monkeys. His only companionship was a plush orangutan gifted to him by zoo staff.

The perseverance, patience and proactive behaviour displayed by little Punch also reminds me of another kindred spirit, the emperor penguin Popi. 
[Popi](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/V8s9yR8jARA?si=lUwE8NxvFUMilJga) rose to fame in the National Geographic documentary [Secrets of the Penguins](https://www.natgeotv.com/uk/shows/nationalgeographicwild/secrets-of-the-penguins). 
It documents the chick’s journey across the ice after he is abandoned by the group for being small and weak. Yet against all odds, Popi makes it and is reunited with the group.

For me there are many parallels in these two stories. Punch and Popi show us what leadership looks like:

- Both Popi and Punch were left alone and abandoned by their groups, their families, when they were young and vulnerable in harsh and hostile environments.

- Both needed to learn how to navigate the world facing social rejection. Yet both move forward instinctively. The law of the jungle is, after all, that you need to move forward to survive.

- Both demonstrate patience, not getting upset or angry despite their conditions and their treatment. This is surprising considering the physical, emotional and mental strain they must have endured.

- Proactivity. Punch did not hide. He explored his enclosure and observed fellow monkeys. This allowed him to learn socially acceptable behaviour, how to initiate social contact and find acceptance. Popi, on the other hand, moves slower than the group but they are always in sight. Popi does not wait for rescue and does not give up. He switches his approach from waddling to sliding, navigating the environment. Both Punch and Popi take charge to ensure change in their situations.

- Both display leadership through gentleness. Despite the conditions they show courage in adversity and maintain kindness despite hardship.

Their gentle determination and quiet heroism of simply continuing is what grasped the hearts of so many people. From the smallest and most vulnerable we learn to cling to hope.

Punch and Popi’s stories and their qualities mirror what researchers must cultivate. So maybe the only PhD that matters is Peace, Hope and Determination.

The human‑centred skills we have pushed aside, crowded out by the illusion of connection created through social media, are perhaps the very skills the world now urgently needs. Interpersonal communication, attentive listening and genuine curiosity about others, so brilliantly demonstrated by these two parallel hearts, are not “soft” skills. They are leadership skills.

Doctoral programmes often claim to cultivate future leaders, yet too often they focus solely on technical expertise and intellectual output. But to lead change, doctoral students need more than analytical ability. Doctoral programmes must shift to value these qualities. Programmes should provide opportunity for PhD students to develop the capacity to guide others and themselves with hope. This will instil the ability to learn to reflect, to evaluate wisely and to imagine a better future for all, despite the current conditions. Essentially, we must encourage researchers and academics to become movers and shakers of sound judgement, acting with both heart and mind for the good of others.

A PhD programme should not only produce deep thinkers. It should nurture leaders, people equipped to solve problems across borders, status and economies with compassion, courage and vision.

The final punchline is then maybe that to foster change and to inspire hope we do not have to be strong, and we do not have to be smart. Rather it begins with the quiet, steady determination to stay kind, stay connected and keep moving forward, just as Punch and Popi did.
